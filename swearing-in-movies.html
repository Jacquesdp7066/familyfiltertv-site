Swearing in movies: how it started and how much there is

American studio films were banned from using profanity from 1934 until a ratings system replaced the ban on 1 November 1968. Two years later, M*A*S*H became, by its director’s account, the first major studio film to use the F-word. Today the heaviest films use it hundreds of times, but the typical film is far milder than the records suggest.

A short history of swearing on screen

YearWhat happened
1934–1968The Production Code, often called the Hays Code, bans profanity outright in American studio films.
1939Gone with the Wind keeps Rhett Butler’s “I don’t give a damn”. The Code forbade the word, so producer David O. Selznick lobbied for an exemption because it was in the novel.
1 November 1968The Motion Picture Association’s ratings system takes over from the Code. Studios can now release films with stronger language, labelled by rating.
1970M*A*S*H is called the first mainstream American studio film to say the F-word. Director Robert Altman made that claim on the DVD commentary. The word comes near the end, during the football game.
SinceSwearing in films increases significantly over the following decades, and a few films are built around it.

“First” depends on what counts: the claim is about major American studios. Films from smaller companies and other countries may have used the word earlier.

How much swearing is normal?

The records describe a handful of extremes. We have counted flagged words in the English subtitles of 1,286 films. A flagged word is any word in our dictionary, from mild language such as damn and religious exclamations such as oh my god up to strong profanity. So these numbers are higher than a count of the F-word alone.

  • Half the films have 43 or fewer flagged words. The average is higher, at about 74, because a few very heavy films pull it up.
  • 55 films, about 4%, have none at all. 184 have five or fewer.
  • 306 films, about one in four, have 100 or more. 120 have 200 or more, and the heaviest has 837.

Among the films we have written up in detail, the heaviest are Uncut Gems, Reservoir Dogs, Knocked Up and Training Day. At the other end, Moana has no flagged words at all. Barbie, for instance, has strong profanity in only one ten-minute stretch, while Bullet Train has it in every one. Browse them all on the films page.

Why films use swearing

Filmmakers usually give three reasons: realism, because people swear in everyday speech and under stress; character, because it can mark someone as rebellious, rough or stressed; and emphasis, for a joke or a moment of confrontation. We can’t measure intent, only how often words appear and where, which is what the film pages show.

Questions

When was swearing first allowed in Hollywood movies?

Not until the late 1960s. The Production Code, in force from 1934 to 1968, banned profanity in American studio films, with rare exceptions such as the word damn in Gone with the Wind (1939), which producer David O. Selznick won an exemption for. The ratings system that replaced the Code on 1 November 1968 let studios release films with stronger language, marked by their rating.

What was the first movie to use the F-word?

It depends on what counts. Director Robert Altman said M*A*S*H (1970) was the first major American studio film to use the word, and the Wikipedia list of films that most use it describes M*A*S*H as the first mainstream American film under the ratings system to do so. Films from outside the major studios and from other countries may have used the word earlier.

Which movie has the most swearing?

By reported count of the F-word, Swearnet: The Movie (2014) leads with 935, and the documentary Fuck (2005) follows with 857. Among narrative feature films, The Wolf of Wall Street (2013) is listed at 569, though sources give between 506 and 569. In our own subtitle analysis of 1,286 films, the heaviest has 837 flagged words of all kinds.

How much swearing is normal in a movie?

Less than the famous records suggest. In 1,286 films we counted from their English subtitles, half have 43 or fewer flagged words, 4% have none, and about one in four has 100 or more. Flagged words include mild language such as damn and religious exclamations, not only strong profanity.
