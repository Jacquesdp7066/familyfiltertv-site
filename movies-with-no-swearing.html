Movies with no swearing: the films we counted with zero flagged words

Of the 1,286 films we have counted, 55 have no flagged words at all in their English subtitles. That is about 4%. Going by the genre tags in the catalogue listing, 35 of the 55 are animated films, and 12 were made before 1960. The list is below. Zero here is a word count, not a rating, so read what it does and does not mean before you press play.

What “zero flagged words” means

  • It counts words in the English subtitles of one release. Another cut, dub or subtitle file of the same film can differ, and the subtitles can leave a word out. A word that is bleeped, mumbled or missing from the subtitle file is not counted.
  • It is spoken language only. We did not assess violence, nudity, frightening scenes or themes. The list includes some horror films, for example, so it is not a list of films for young children.
  • It is not a rating. A flagged word is any word in our dictionary, from mild language such as damn and religious exclamations up to strong profanity, and zero means the dictionary found none.
  • Films in other languages are counted from their English subtitles, which are a translation, so a count for those films is only as good as that translation. How we count swear words has the full rules.

Films from before 1960

12 of the 55 zero films and 17 of the almost-clean films were made before 1960, which is why we keep them out of the main tables. Several were made in Hollywood under the Production Code, which banned profanity in American studio films from 1934 to 1968, so a clean count is expected. It does not make them a recommendation, and it would be wrong to count them as a sign of today’s films.

No flagged words: Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937); Citizen Kane (1941); The Maltese Falcon (1941); Double Indemnity (1944); Notorious (1946); All About Eve (1950); Sunset Boulevard (1950); A Streetcar Named Desire (1951); High Noon (1952); Tokyo Story (1953); Dial M for Murder (1954); Vertigo (1958).

How this compares with the average film

Zero is rare. In the 1,286 films we counted, half have 43 or fewer flagged words, about 4% have none and about one in four has 100 or more. So a film on the list above is far from typical, and a film with five or fewer is still in the cleanest one in seven. The swearing in movies page has the history and the wider numbers, and the films page has a full breakdown for every film we have written up in detail, including Moana, Toy Story and The Lego Movie.

Questions

Which movies have no swearing?

Of the 1,286 films we have analysed, 55 have no flagged words in their English subtitles, including Moana (2016), Toy Story (1995), The Lego Movie (2014), Finding Dory (2016) and Mary Poppins (1964). The full list is on this page. Zero means none in the subtitles of the one release we counted. It is not a rating, and it says nothing about violence or frightening scenes.

Does zero flagged words mean a movie is suitable for kids?

No. It only means our word list found nothing in the English subtitles of that release. We did not assess violence, nudity, frightening scenes or themes, so a film with no flagged words can still be unsuitable for young children. The list includes horror films, for example. Check an age rating or a parents guide for those.

How much swearing does a typical movie have?

In the 1,286 films we counted, half have 43 or fewer flagged words. About 4% have none, 14% have five or fewer, and about one in four has 100 or more. Flagged words include mild language such as damn and religious exclamations, not only strong profanity.

What counts as a flagged word?

Any word in our dictionary found in a film's English subtitles, filed under strong profanity, mild profanity, religious exclamations, crude language, explicit sexual language, suggestive language or slurs. Every appearance counts, and bleeped or unsubtitled words cannot be counted. The rules are on our methodology page.

Can I watch other movies with the swearing muted?

It works with supported Stremio playback and follows along with Netflix and other apps. It covers spoken language only, English titles for now, and muting is best effort, so a word may slip through. Sources and method

  • Our own counts of flagged words in the English subtitles of 1,286 films, up to 12 October 2026, the same set as the comparisons on our film pages. We use only films with a rated analysis, a subtitle timing that matches the running time, at least 400 subtitle lines, and no commentary, trailer or sample release. See how we count swear words.
  • Years come from the release name of the subtitle file we analysed. Where the name has no year we used the year in the film’s catalogue listing (Cinemeta, the metadata service Stremio uses). Where the two disagreed we left the year out rather than choose. We left out Anne of Green Gables, which has no year in its listing and shares its title with several adaptations. We checked the listing’s genre tags, and none of the films above is tagged as a documentary or a concert.
  • Facts checked 15 October 2026. We keep adding films, so the full list will grow.
